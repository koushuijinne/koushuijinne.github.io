欧洲人的隐私：从文化根源到工程落地

European Privacy: From Cultural Roots to Engineering Practice

作者：Julien ｜ 华为数字能源，光储 AI 调度算法工程师
By Julien — AI dispatch-algorithm engineer, PV + storage systems

缘起 · Where This Started

中文

我在华为数字能源部门做算法工程师，主要负责欧洲光伏储能系统的 AI 调度算法优化。

工作中有个反复出现的困扰：欧洲用户遇到调度问题、需要技术方给出解释时会来找我们，但我们身在中国，却常常因为隐私合规无法直接获取用户数据。

这让我作为一个土生土长的中国人产生了一个疑问——欧美人，尤其是欧洲人，为什么如此看重隐私？

这个疑问在很多地方都能遇到它的影子。区块链圈子里，以太坊把零知识证明与隐私保护列为发展重心；不少欧美观察者认为公共空间的高密度摄像头侵犯公民隐私；Apple 把隐私保护做成核心卖点。而在我所在的光储行业，中国工程师往往觉得发电量、用电量不过是无害的工程数据，可在欧洲，它同样被当作需要保护的隐私。

这个问题不只关乎消费者心理，也考验企业的合规基本功，背后还牵涉到深层的文化差异。带着疑问，我做了一轮系统的资料梳理，整理出下面这条从”为什么在乎”到”如何落地”的完整链条，分享给同样在出海、或对跨文化议题感兴趣的读者。

English

I work as an algorithm engineer at Huawei Digital Energy, focused on optimizing AI dispatch algorithms for photovoltaic-plus-storage systems in Europe.

One problem kept recurring. When European users hit a dispatch issue and asked our technical team to explain it, they came to us — yet because we sit in China, privacy compliance often blocked us from directly accessing their data.

As someone born and raised in China, this raised a genuine question: why do people in Europe, in particular, care so intensely about privacy?

The question echoes across many domains. In the blockchain world, Ethereum treats zero-knowledge proofs and privacy as a strategic priority. Many Western observers regard dense public surveillance cameras as an intrusion on civic privacy. Apple built privacy into its core value proposition. And in my own field, Chinese engineers tend to see generation and consumption figures as harmless engineering data — while in Europe, the same numbers are treated as protected personal information.

This is not only about consumer psychology. It tests a company’s compliance fundamentals, and it rests on deeper cultural differences. So I worked through the question systematically and assembled the chain below — from why it matters to how it is actually engineered — for readers who are also expanding abroad, or who simply find cross-cultural questions interesting.

一、为什么在乎：历史与哲学的双重根源

Part 1 — Why It Matters: Historical and Philosophical Roots

历史创伤 · Historical Trauma

中文

欧洲对隐私的高度重视，一个核心驱动力来自 20 世纪中叶两次极权统治留下的集体创伤。

第一个例子是纳粹时期的人口普查。20 世纪 30 年代，纳粹德国借助当时先进的霍列瑞斯制表机（由 IBM 的德国子公司 Dehomag 提供）进行高效人口统计，详细记录了公民的宗教、血统、职业等信息。这些看似中立的”个人数据”，后来为精确识别和搜捕特定群体提供了数据基础。历史学者 Edwin Black 在《IBM 与大屠杀》中对这一链条有系统的记录。

第二个例子是战后东德的国家安全局（斯塔西）。它建立了庞大的密探与告密网络，对普通公民的日常生活进行全方位监控，档案与线人渗透到家庭与友谊之中。

这两段经历沉淀为一个共识：个人数据不只是商业资产或管理工具，它直接关系到人身安全与人格尊严。一旦权力机构掌握了公民的全部信息，极权的风险就并非抽象假设。正因如此，欧洲法律（如 GDPR）将政治倾向、宗教信仰、性取向、基因数据等列为”特殊类别数据”，给予最高级别保护。

English

A central driver of Europe’s emphasis on privacy is the collective trauma left by two mid-twentieth-century totalitarian regimes.

The first example is the Nazi-era census. In the 1930s, Nazi Germany used the then-advanced Hollerith tabulating machines — supplied by IBM’s German subsidiary Dehomag — to run efficient population counts that recorded citizens’ religion, ancestry, and occupation in detail. Those seemingly neutral “personal data” later provided a foundation for identifying and locating targeted groups. The historian Edwin Black documented this chain in IBM and the Holocaust.

The second example is the Stasi, the state security service of postwar East Germany. It built a vast network of agents and informants that surveilled ordinary citizens’ daily lives, with files and informers reaching into families and friendships.

These experiences hardened into a shared conviction: personal data is not merely a commercial asset or a management tool — it bears directly on physical safety and human dignity. Once an authority holds the entirety of a citizen’s information, the risk of authoritarian overreach is no longer abstract. This is why European law such as the GDPR designates political opinions, religious beliefs, sexual orientation, and genetic data as “special category data,” subject to the highest level of protection.

哲学分野 · Philosophical Divergence

中文

在文化与哲学层面，欧洲与美国等西方国家对”什么是隐私”以及”主要威胁来自谁”有着不同理解。

其一是隐私即人权 vs 隐私即自由/财产。欧洲（尤其法德等大陆法系国家）深受康德等哲学传统影响，把隐私视为人格尊严不可分割的一部分，属于基本人权。即便个人愿意为了便利让渡隐私，法律也会像保护生命权一样，限制个人过度出让隐私。美国则更倾向把隐私理解为一种自由或财产权，倾向在商业框架下进行利益交换。

其二是防御对象的不同。美国基于其反暴政的建国传统，更警惕政府对隐私的侵犯，对商业公司的数据索取相对宽容；欧洲则同样警惕商业巨头（如硅谷科技公司）对个人生活的资本化与掌控，认为资本对个人数据的滥用同样是对人格尊严的侵蚀。

理解这两条分野，就能理解后文里欧盟一系列做法的动机——它既要防政府，也要防平台。

English

At the cultural and philosophical level, Europe and other Western countries such as the United States understand what privacy is and where the main threat comes from differently.

The first divide is privacy as a human right versus privacy as liberty or property. Europe — especially civil-law countries like France and Germany — draws on a Kantian tradition that treats privacy as an inseparable part of human dignity, and therefore a fundamental right. Even when an individual is willing to trade away privacy for convenience, the law limits over-alienation of that privacy, much as it protects the right to life. The United States leans toward seeing privacy as a form of liberty or property, more readily exchanged within a commercial framework.

The second divide concerns who the defense is against. Grounded in an anti-tyranny founding tradition, the United States is more wary of government intrusion and comparatively tolerant of corporate data collection. Europe is equally wary of large corporations — Silicon Valley platforms among them — capitalizing on and controlling personal life, viewing corporate misuse of personal data as an erosion of dignity in its own right.

Holding these two divides in mind makes the motivation behind the EU measures discussed later legible: they are designed to check both governments and platforms.

二、数据为什么敏感：一份”伪装成工程量”的行为记录

Part 2 — Why the Data Is Sensitive: A Behavioral Record Disguised as Engineering Metrics

中文

回到我自己的行业。在中国工程师的直觉里，发电量和用电量只是无害的工程数据。但欧洲监管把高分辨率负荷曲线当作行为监控数据，只是它伪装成了工程参数。这一判断，恰恰是我理解整件事的转折点。

这个领域有个成熟方向，叫非侵入式负荷监测（NILM）。它最初是为节能设计的：从单点总表的读数里，把各个电器的启停分解出来，帮住户看清用电结构。Hart 在 1992 年的奠基性论文奠定了这套方法，此后隐马尔可夫模型、深度学习相继成为主流手段。

问题在于它的能力边界。当前大规模部署的智能电表，典型采样粒度是 15 分钟一次——就这个粒度，已经足以看出你几点起床、几点回家、周末在不在家、家里大概几口人、什么时候度假外出。采样率更高时，可以进一步识别到具体电器的运行状态。换句话说，一条负荷曲线，读出来的是居住者作息的连续记录。

对工商业客户，同一份曲线泄露的是产能利用率和生产排程。竞争对手拿到你的负荷曲线，基本等同于拿到了你的开工率。这一层的法律保护基础换成了商业秘密，约束强度不降反升。

所以监管的判断站得住：这份数据看着像工程参数，实质是居住者或经营者行为的连续记录，其敏感度接近位置数据，只是不那么直观。理解了这一层，前面那个”为什么在乎”就从抽象的文化命题，落到了我每天打交道的具体数据上。

English

Back to my own field. In a Chinese engineer’s intuition, generation and consumption figures are harmless engineering data. But European regulators treat a high-resolution load curve as behavioral surveillance data — merely disguised as engineering metrics. That judgment was the turning point in how I understood the whole matter.

There is a mature research direction here called non-intrusive load monitoring (NILM). It was originally designed for energy saving: from a single whole-house meter reading, it disaggregates the on/off activity of individual appliances so residents can see where their electricity goes. Hart’s foundational 1992 paper established the approach; hidden Markov models and later deep learning became the dominant methods.

The problem is where its capability reaches. The typical sampling granularity for today’s mass-deployed smart meters is once every 15 minutes — and at that granularity alone, one can already infer when you wake up, when you get home, whether you are away on weekends, roughly how many people live in the home, and when you leave for vacation. At higher sampling rates, the operating state of specific appliances can be identified further. In other words, a load curve reads out as a continuous record of a resident’s daily rhythm.

For commercial and industrial customers, the same curve leaks capacity utilization and production scheduling. A competitor holding your load curve effectively holds your operating rate. Here the legal basis of protection shifts to trade secrets, and the constraints tighten rather than loosen.

So the regulatory judgment holds: the data looks like engineering parameters but is in substance a continuous record of a resident’s or operator’s behavior, with a sensitivity close to location data, only less obvious. Once this clicked, the earlier question of “why they care” moved from an abstract cultural proposition down to the concrete data I handle every day.

三、工程上怎么落地：技术售后如何在拿不到数据的情况下工作

Part 3 — How It Is Engineered: Supporting Customers Without Holding Their Data

中文

理解了敏感性，真正的工程问题就浮现了：售后需要排障，却不能直接看用户原始数据，这个矛盾怎么解？金融、SaaS、云服务这些数据合规要求最高的行业，已经沉淀出一套成熟做法。下面四层，是我梳理下来觉得最实用的部分。

第一层：边缘脱敏 / 令牌化

做法是在数据离开所在区域之前，就把敏感字段拦下来，替换成稳定的占位符，售后看到的永远是结构化的假值。有一条原则说得很清楚：任何包含或能关联到客户标识的东西，只写入本区域存储；而在区域内算出的聚合指标（计数、时延、错误率、质量分）才允许跨区到中央看板。

关键细节是确定性脱敏——同一个 ID 每次都映射成同一个哈希值。这样系统在测试环境里行为可预测，售后能照常排障，但拿不到原值。金融行业用的令牌化更进一步：数据默认以令牌形态存储，只有明确需要时才在受控条件下还原。

第二层：诊断产物出境、原始数据留下

核心模式是区域内计算、只出结论。评估样本留在区域内，评分模型在同区域运行，只有分数跨区到看板。行业把它总结成一条可操作的规则：能跨区的只有区域内算出的聚合量，任何能反查到客户的原始记录只写本地。

第三层：即时访问 + 破窗流程

当诊断结论不够、售后确实需要看真实入参时，两套机制配合使用。

即时访问（Just-in-Time, JIT）：售后默认没有生产数据权限，报障时按工单申请，系统发放短时效、限定范围的临时凭证——绑定本次任务、有效期数分钟、任务结束即失效、超出范围不可用。有些实现还会按地理位置判权：请求者所在地不符合策略就不予批准。

破窗流程（Break-glass）：几乎每个 SRE 组织都有破窗流程，但很少有人量化它的代价。核心是紧急情况下允许临时提权，但每一个动作全程留痕——谁激活、何时激活、访问了哪些资源、做了什么操作，日志安全存储并定期复核。合规审计员会检查日志里是否残留原始个人数据，会追问数据如何脱敏与令牌化，并核实这些控制是否实时生效，而不是只在批量导出时做做样子。

第四层：区域内密钥托管

这是数据驻留的兜底。主权云的做法是把加密密钥留在管辖区内：根密钥存放在区域内的硬件安全模块，跨区使用需要策略例外和显式的破窗批准。这样即便数据被复制出境，在许可边界之外也无法解密。

这四层叠起来，回答了我最初的困惑——不是”拿不到数据所以没法做售后”，而是整个行业已经把”在不接触原始数据的前提下仍能排障”变成了一套标准工程实践。

English

Once you understand the sensitivity, the real engineering problem surfaces: support needs to troubleshoot, yet cannot directly view a user’s raw data. How is that contradiction resolved? Industries with the strictest data-compliance requirements — finance, SaaS, cloud — have settled on a mature playbook. The four layers below are the parts I found most practical.

Layer 1 — Edge redaction / tokenization

Sensitive fields are intercepted before data leaves its home region and replaced with stable placeholders, so support always sees structured but fake values. One principle states it plainly: anything that contains or can be linked to a customer identifier is written only to in-region storage, while aggregate metrics computed within the region (counts, latency, error rates, quality scores) are the only things allowed to cross regions to a central dashboard.

The key detail is deterministic redaction — the same ID always maps to the same hash. This keeps system behavior predictable in test environments so support can troubleshoot as usual, without ever obtaining the original value. Finance goes a step further with tokenization: data is stored as tokens by default and restored only under controlled conditions when explicitly required.

Layer 2 — Export the diagnosis, keep the raw data

The core pattern is compute in region, emit only conclusions. Evaluation samples stay in region, the scoring model runs in the same region, and only the score crosses to the dashboard. The industry distills this into an operable rule: only in-region aggregates may cross regions; any raw record traceable back to a customer is written locally.

Layer 3 — Just-in-Time access + break-glass

When the diagnosis is insufficient and support genuinely needs to see real inputs, two mechanisms work together.

Just-in-Time (JIT) access: support holds no production-data privileges by default. On a ticket, the system issues a short-lived, tightly scoped temporary credential — bound to this task, valid for minutes, expiring when the task ends, unusable outside its scope. Some implementations also gate on geography: if the requester’s location does not match policy, the request is denied.

Break-glass: nearly every SRE organization has a break-glass procedure, though few quantify its cost. The core idea is that emergencies permit temporary privilege escalation, but every action is logged end to end — who activated it, when, which resources were accessed, what was done — with logs stored securely and reviewed periodically. Compliance auditors check whether raw personal data lingers in logs, ask how data is redacted and tokenized, and verify that these controls take effect in real time rather than being applied only for show during batch exports.

Layer 4 — In-region key custody

This is the backstop for data residency. Sovereign clouds keep encryption keys inside the jurisdiction: root keys live in an in-region hardware security module, and cross-region use requires a policy exception and explicit break-glass approval. So even if data is copied out, it cannot be decrypted beyond the permitted boundary.

Stacked together, these four layers answer my original puzzle. It is not that “we can’t get the data, so we can’t do support.” Rather, the industry has already turned “troubleshooting without touching raw data” into a standard engineering practice.

四、未来技术方向：让”证明”取代”披露”

Part 4 — The Technical Frontier: Replacing Disclosure with Proof

中文

前三部分讲的是”如何保护已经收集的数据”。而真正的技术前沿，是从源头上让数据不必被披露——用密码学证明取代原始数据的交换。这一层，作为区块链技术爱好者，我格外感兴趣。

零知识证明

零知识证明是一种密码学技术，允许证明者向验证者证明”某个断言成立”，而不泄露任何多余信息。

一个经典比喻：我想证明我知道你家大门密码，但不想告诉你密码是多少。我当着你的面，用密码把门打开、走进去、再走出来——这就证明了”我知道密码”，同时”没有泄露密码”。

在区块链中，它主要用于两件事：隐私（隐藏交易金额与地址）和扩容（ZK-Rollup，如 Starknet、zkSync，把成千上万笔交易打包成一个极小的证明上链）。

它带来的是合规友好的隐私，而非绝对保密。现代 ZK 隐私更多是”选择性披露”：银行或机构想证明自己合规、证明储备、做私密结算，同时又不公开敏感数据。监管要透明，用户要机密，零知识证明让两者有机共存。

顺带一提，ZK 与密码学工程被普遍认为是当前软件工程里最稀缺的技能之一：人才池极小、需求巨大，而常规计算机课程通常不教构造 SNARK/STARK 所需的数学。它在区块链行业撑起扩容、隐私、验证三大板块，天花板高、也最难被自动化替代。（具体薪资区间各来源差异较大，此处不做精确断言。）

自主权身份（Self-Sovereign Identity, SSI）

自主权身份是一种重构数字身份控制权的数据架构。

传统 Web2 模式依赖中心化机构（互联网平台、政府数据库）托管数据与验证身份，导致用户缺乏对自身数据的绝对控制权，并面临集中式数据泄露的风险。SSI 通过去中心化手段，把数据的持有权与控制权解耦，让用户自主管理、存储并授权使用自己的身份凭证。

在 W3C DID 规范的标准模型里，信任由三个实体协同完成：

  • 发行方（Issuer）：拥有权威数据源的机构（政府、教育、金融机构）。它用私钥对用户的身份属性（出生日期、学位等）做密码学签名，生成可验证凭证（Verifiable Credential, VC）发给用户。
  • 持有方（Holder）：身份的实际主体，也就是用户本人。用户不把原始数据上传到云端或第三方，而是通过本地加密钱包独立存储凭证。
  • 验证方（Verifier）：需要查验某项属性的业务方。它不直接对接发行方，而是用密码学算法直接校验持有方出示证明的真伪。

为了避免验证时过度暴露数据，SSI 主要依赖两项技术：

选择性披露（Selective Disclosure）。传统数字证书（如 X.509）具有全包裹性，出示时必须暴露内部所有字段。选择性披露（如基于 BBS+ 签名方案）允许持有方对凭证内的字段选择性隐匿。例如验证年龄时，用户只披露”年龄 ≥ 18”这一布尔值，而把姓名、住址、证件号等字段在密码学层面屏蔽。

零知识证明。它允许持有方在不泄露属性明文的前提下，证明该属性满足某种代数关系（如范围证明、成员归属证明）。标准业务时序大致是：验证方提出请求 → 持有方本地设备调取明文凭证作为私密输入，在算术电路里计算，输出一段固定长度、不含任何明文的证明 → 验证方用发行方的公钥验算，等式成立即通过。整个过程，敏感明文从未离开用户设备。

欧盟的执着：eIDAS 2.0

欧盟不仅在推进这套技术，还把它上升到了法律和强制执行的层面。

这项法案是 eIDAS 2.0（Regulation (EU) 2024/1183），于 2024 年 5 月 20 日正式生效。根据其时间表，到 2026 年底，每个欧盟成员国都必须向公民、居民和企业提供至少一款经认证的”欧盟数字身份钱包”（EUDI Wallet），且多数官方表述强调免费提供。到 2027 年底，银行、电信、医疗等受监管行业以及超大型在线平台，需强制接受该钱包作为强身份认证方式。

技术底座上，欧盟全面采用了前面提到的自主权身份、W3C 可验证凭证，以及选择性披露（基于 SD-JWT 等技术，可平滑过渡到零知识证明）这套栈。

推进它的动机可以拆成三层：

  • 战略层——打破身份垄断。如今互联网上几乎所有”一键登录”都被少数美国科技巨头掌握。欧盟希望通过官方钱包，让公民跳过这些账号，直接用政府认证的数字身份登录全欧公共服务、银行乃至大型电商。按法案，超大型在线平台未来被要求必须支持该钱包登录。
  • 体验层——统一的无缝数字市场。欧盟各国数字基础设施此前各自为政，跨国办事极其繁琐。钱包的目标是”一次认证、全欧通用”：一个德国学生去西班牙留学，钱包里德国大学颁发的学位、政府颁发的证明（均为 W3C 可验证凭证形式），可以被西班牙的学校和房东用密码学瞬间跨国验证，不再需要繁琐的公证与双认证。
  • 隐私层——消灭”过度索取”。现有验证（买酒、租车）往往要出示整张身份证，对方顺便看光了你的姓名、证件号和住址。选择性披露允许你买酒时只提供一个”已满 18 岁”的密码学证明，其余数据一概隐藏。

不过需要如实补充一点：这套时间表理想，落地进度参差。2026 年年中的行业跟踪显示，达到就绪基准的成员国还不到三分之一，部分国家已释放出首发功能有限、甚至延迟的信号（如荷兰、马耳他被提及）。德国等则在按大规模用户量推进沙箱与实施。所以它是”正在发生、但尚未完成”的现实，而非既成事实——这个理想与落地之间的落差，本身就是观察这类跨国工程最有价值的地方。

English

The first three parts covered how to protect data already collected. The real frontier is to make disclosure unnecessary at the source — replacing the exchange of raw data with cryptographic proof. As a blockchain enthusiast, this layer interests me most.

Zero-knowledge proofs

A zero-knowledge proof is a cryptographic technique that lets a prover convince a verifier that “some statement is true” without revealing any additional information.

A classic analogy: I want to prove I know the code to your front door, without telling you what the code is. In front of you, I use the code to open the door, step inside, and step back out — proving I know the code while revealing nothing about it.

In blockchain, it serves two main purposes: privacy (hiding transaction amounts and addresses) and scaling (ZK-Rollups such as Starknet and zkSync, which pack tens of thousands of transactions into a single tiny proof posted on chain).

What it delivers is compliance-friendly privacy, not absolute secrecy. Modern ZK privacy is largely “selective disclosure”: a bank or institution wants to prove it is compliant, prove reserves, or settle privately, without exposing sensitive data. Regulators want transparency; users want confidentiality; zero-knowledge proofs let the two coexist.

As an aside, ZK and cryptography engineering are widely regarded as among the scarcest skills in software today: the talent pool is tiny, demand is large, and standard computer-science curricula generally do not teach the mathematics needed to construct SNARKs and STARKs. It underpins three pillars of the blockchain field — scaling, privacy, and verification — with a high ceiling and strong resistance to automation. (Specific salary ranges vary widely across sources, so I make no precise claim here.)

Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI)

Self-sovereign identity is a data architecture that reconstructs control over digital identity.

The traditional Web2 model relies on centralized institutions — platforms, government databases — to custody data and verify identity, leaving users without absolute control over their own data and exposed to centralized breaches. SSI uses decentralized techniques to decouple possession from control, letting users independently manage, store, and authorize the use of their own identity credentials.

In the standard W3C DID model, trust is established by three cooperating parties:

  • Issuer: an authority holding a source of truth (government, education, financial institution). It cryptographically signs a user’s identity attributes (date of birth, degree, etc.) with its private key, producing a Verifiable Credential (VC) issued to the user.
  • Holder: the actual subject — the user. Rather than uploading raw data to the cloud or a third party, the user stores credentials independently in a local encrypted wallet.
  • Verifier: a party needing to check some attribute. It does not contact the issuer directly; it uses cryptography to validate the proof the holder presents.

To avoid over-exposing data during verification, SSI relies on two techniques:

Selective disclosure. A traditional digital certificate (like X.509) is all-or-nothing: presenting it exposes every field inside. Selective disclosure (e.g., via BBS+ signature schemes) lets the holder selectively conceal fields within a credential. Verifying age, for instance, a user discloses only the boolean “age ≥ 18” while cryptographically masking name, address, and ID number.

Zero-knowledge proofs. These let the holder prove an attribute satisfies some algebraic relation (a range proof, a membership proof) without revealing the plaintext. The standard sequence: the verifier issues a request → the holder’s local device pulls the plaintext credential as a private input, computes inside an arithmetic circuit, and outputs a fixed-length proof containing no plaintext → the verifier checks it against the issuer’s public key, and if the equation holds, it passes. Throughout, the sensitive plaintext never leaves the user’s device.

Europe’s commitment: eIDAS 2.0

The EU is not only advancing this technology — it has elevated it to law and enforcement.

The instrument is eIDAS 2.0 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1183), which entered into force on 20 May 2024. Under its timetable, by the end of 2026 every EU member state must provide citizens, residents, and businesses with at least one certified “EU Digital Identity Wallet” (EUDI Wallet) — with most official framing emphasizing that it is offered free of charge. By the end of 2027, regulated sectors such as banking, telecoms, and healthcare, along with very large online platforms, must accept the wallet as a strong-authentication method.

Technically, the EU has embraced the full stack described above: self-sovereign identity, W3C Verifiable Credentials, and selective disclosure (based on technologies like SD-JWT, with a smooth path toward zero-knowledge proofs).

The motivation breaks into three layers:

  • Strategic — breaking the identity monopoly. Today almost every “one-click login” online runs through a handful of American tech giants. Through an official wallet, the EU wants citizens to bypass those accounts and log into pan-European public services, banks, and even large e-commerce using a government-certified digital identity. Under the regulation, very large online platforms will be required to support wallet login.
  • Experience — a unified, seamless digital market. Member states’ digital infrastructures previously operated in isolation, making cross-border tasks painful. The wallet aims for “authenticate once, use everywhere”: a German student studying in Spain can have a German university degree and government-issued documents (all as W3C Verifiable Credentials) verified cross-border in an instant by Spanish schools and landlords, with no cumbersome notarization or dual authentication.
  • Privacy — eliminating over-collection. Existing verification (buying alcohol, renting a car) often requires showing a full ID, letting the other party incidentally see your name, ID number, and address. Selective disclosure lets you present only a cryptographic proof of “over 18” when buying alcohol, concealing everything else.

One honest caveat is worth adding: the timetable is ambitious, but rollout is uneven. Industry tracking in mid-2026 indicated that fewer than one-third of member states met the readiness benchmark, with some signaling limited launch functionality or delays (the Netherlands and Malta have been cited). Others, such as Germany, are pushing sandboxes and implementations toward large user bases. So it is a reality that is “happening but not yet finished,” rather than an accomplished fact — and that gap between the ambition and the ground truth is itself the most instructive part of watching a cross-border engineering effort like this.
